# KneadIt Marketing Design Research

## Direction

KneadIt should market itself as the operating system for a cottage bakery, not as another storefront template. The strongest story is the shift from scattered, improvised business work to one calm daily rhythm: capture the order, prepare the bake, collect payment, keep the customer close, and know the margin.

## What current product marketing gets right

Linear leads with one precise product promise, then demonstrates the product through real interface states and workflow sections rather than relying on abstract feature claims. Its homepage moves from thesis to concrete product surfaces, then into planning, automation, build/review, customer proof, and a direct “get started” path. See [Linear](https://linear.app/).

Loafaly is the closest category reference. It positions against “duct-taping five tools together,” makes setup speed explicit, and shows the connection between storefront, recipe costing, production, customer communication, and marketing. It also uses before/after demonstrations and real product surfaces to make the promise tangible. See [Loafaly](https://www.loafaly.com/).

The broader bakery-software category repeatedly emphasizes business-specific workflows — orders, production, pricing, payments, customers, and storefronts — rather than generic project-management language. That validates KneadIt’s product truth, but also means the page needs a sharper ownable narrative than a checklist of features. See [BakerOS](https://getbakeros.com/) and [BakeStreet](https://bakestreet.co/).

## Additional SaaS landing-page research

The Dribbble references are useful for visual vocabulary, but not as literal layouts. The strongest recurring patterns across the reviewed SaaS hero work are:

- One short, outcome-led promise with a single dominant CTA. Secondary actions are quieter and do not compete with the product story. See [SaaS Marketing Landing Page](https://dribbble.com/shots/26462013-SaaS-Marketing-Landing-Page) and [Responsive B2B SaaS Landing Page](https://dribbble.com/shots/27328378-SaaS-Web-Design-Responsive-B2B-Landing-Page-All-Breakpoints).
- A product surface that is large enough to understand. The visual is usually a coherent screen, workflow, or demo state — not a collection of unrelated cards floating in space. See [SaaS Landing Page — Hero UI](https://dribbble.com/shots/27295954-SaaS-Landing-Page-Hero-UI).
- A restrained visual system: one accent color for action and emphasis, quiet neutrals for the interface, and typography that creates hierarchy without needing extra labels, stickers, or ornamental effects. The broader [Dribbble SaaS landing-page gallery](https://dribbble.com/search/saas-landing-page-ui) and [Land-book SaaS gallery](https://land-book.com/design/website/saas) reinforce this pattern.
- Product evidence early in the page. [One Page Love’s app and SaaS collection](https://onepagelove.com/genre/app) specifically curates real shipped examples rather than concept shots, and the strongest examples use demos, screenshots, or interactive product moments to establish credibility.
- A progression from promise to proof to workflow. The page should not spend the hero on atmosphere and then introduce the product later; it should establish the job-to-be-done, show the product doing it, and then unpack the workflow below. [Lapa Ninja’s SaaS collection](https://www.lapa.ninja/category/saas/) describes the same conversion sequence across its curated examples.

## Screenshot diagnosis

The current screenshot exposes a few concrete problems that are more important than changing the color palette:

1. The personalized headline is not contained. “Your way” collides with the right-hand panel, which makes the layout feel unfinished at the exact moment the page needs confidence.
2. The right-hand hero visual has a large amount of empty dark space and only a few floating cards. It looks like a mood board or dashboard illustration rather than a usable product.
3. The cards, circular badge, handwritten note, and faux labels compete for attention without explaining one complete baker workflow.
4. The visual language is trying to be playful in several unrelated ways at once. That makes the page feel younger and more template-like, not more distinctive.

## Recommended hero direction

Replace the current “digital kitchen counter” collage with **One workday, one screen**:

- Left: a controlled, two- or three-line promise such as “Run the business behind every bake.” Support it with one specific sentence about orders, production, payments, and margins. Keep “Start your free trial” as the clear primary CTA.
- Right: one large, polished KneadIt product surface titled “Today’s bake.” It should show a believable order queue, a production timeline, payment status, and a small margin/profit signal. Use real interface density and alignment; no random floating cards.
- Above the product surface: a tiny contextual cue such as “Saturday · 5:30 AM” only if it reinforces the day-in-the-life story. It should not become a decorative label system.
- Behind the product surface: a quiet flour/cream field or a single warm bakery texture, not a second competing composition. The product remains the focal point.
- Keep the cocoa, flour, toasted orange, and butter-yellow palette, but use butter-yellow only for action and meaningful state. The interface itself should be mostly cream, ink, and muted warm neutrals.
- Remove the handwritten slogan, circular badge, rotated paper cards, faux browser chrome, and decorative pseudo-labels. Distinction should come from the product story, typography, and the quality of the demo surface.

This gives the page a more mature SaaS posture while preserving the original KneadIt ideas: cottage bakery specificity, a baker’s day, warmth, and the promise of getting the business out of the baker’s head. It also gives us a clear standard for the next implementation: if a visual element does not explain the work being done, it does not belong in the hero.

## Design conclusions

1. Start with the business tension, not the feature inventory: the baker is already good at baking; the problem is holding the business together around it.
2. Show the product early and at useful scale. A dashboard, order flow, recipe-costing view, and storefront preview are more persuasive than decorative bakery imagery alone.
3. Use the “baker’s day” as the page’s narrative device. It turns a long feature list into a familiar operating rhythm.
4. Create one memorable before/after moment: scattered customer notes, recipe details, and payment follow-ups resolve into one connected KneadIt workspace.
5. Keep the brand warm and appetizing through cocoa, flour, toasted orange, and butter yellow. Avoid green as a primary brand anchor.
6. Do not invent customer logos, usage metrics, testimonials, or performance claims. KneadIt currently has product demonstrations and brand assets, but no verified external proof in the repository.

## Implementation plan

- Keep the personalized bakery-name hero interaction.
- Keep the baker’s-day timeline, pricing, FAQ, story, and contact flow.
- Add a visual “scattered to sorted” transition immediately after the hero proof strip.
- Make the dashboard feel like a product demonstration, not a static illustration.
- Use restrained motion for the transition and product surfaces, with a reduced-motion fallback.
- Keep the primary action consistent: “Start your free trial.”
